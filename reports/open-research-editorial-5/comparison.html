Cab Calloway Gets His Big Number Back

By the time Cab Calloway sings “Minnie the Moocher” in The Blues Brothers, the song is nearly half a century old. The 1980 film places Calloway in white, fronting a band on a deliberately theatrical set, and gives his signature number a new room to command.

That distinction matters. “Minnie the Moocher” had been Calloway’s signature since the early 1930s, but John Landis’s film does not present the number as a sepia flashback. The production reportedly resisted Calloway’s idea for a disco variation and steered the scene toward a big-band arrangement instead. Calloway’s entrance, the band’s formal line, and the camera’s attention to his body turn the performance into a handoff between eras: the film’s young revivalists make room for the man who already knew how to command a crowd.

The performance therefore does useful historical work. It brings Calloway’s best-known number into a 1980 film without sanding away its theatricality. Minnie is the point: a song old enough to be history, staged as if it had just walked through the door.

One File, Two Buddy Holly Songs

The useful fact about this Buddy Holly pairing is hiding in plain sight: it names two songs. “Not Fade Away” and “Peggy Sue” are not alternate titles for one performance, and the 1956 date attached to the program cannot safely serve as the release year for either. Both songs are generally documented as 1957 recordings.

That makes the pairing a small lesson in how rock history gets packaged. “Not Fade Away” belongs to Holly and the Crickets and carries a co-writing credit associated with Norman Petty; “Peggy Sue” is tied to Holly, Jerry Allison, and Petty, with the song’s title famously connected to Allison’s girlfriend. Later compilations routinely place such signatures beside one another, turning a career into a neat shelf.

The performance context complicates the neatness in a useful way. A period microphone and sung delivery put the two songs in the same frame of attention, but the exact source and performer are not established here. The right story is not a forced identity. It is the collision of two songs—and the warning that a compilation date is not a recording date.

Rhapsody in Blue Was Built to Be Recast

“Rhapsody in Blue” arrived with a famous name attached to it, but not with a single permanent sound. George Gershwin’s piece was commissioned by Paul Whiteman and premiered in 1924 with Gershwin at the piano. Ferde Grofé orchestrated the accompaniment, and later versions helped turn the work into something many listeners recognize as a full concert showpiece.

In a monochrome performance, a featured clarinetist stands before a large ensemble: piano in the foreground, brass and rhythm instruments behind him, the arrangement translating Gershwin’s blue notes into a staged band performance. The specific orchestra and program remain unidentified, but the musical situation is clear enough: this is a work built to move between jazz, concert, recording, and broadcast settings.

That uncertainty does not weaken the subject. It points to the central story. “Rhapsody in Blue” has survived partly by being recast—by Whiteman, by orchestral arrangers, by bands, by broadcasters, and by performers who keep negotiating the border between jazz and the concert hall. The missing name is less important than the visible argument: this music keeps changing clothes without losing its outline.

“Banned Cartoons” Is a Question, Not a Title

“Vintage Banned Cartoons” opens onto a real but unruly history. Animation has been altered by changing standards, local censorship, syndication decisions, and later attempts to keep offensive material out of ordinary television circulation. The category is broad because the mechanisms are broad: a work can be withdrawn, edited, blocked by a distributor, or simply become difficult to see. Those conditions are not interchangeable.

There are documented examples. The Censored Eleven were Warner Bros. shorts withdrawn from syndication in 1968 because of racist portrayals. That history matters, but it belongs to those identified films; it cannot be transferred to every vintage cartoon that later receives a “banned” label.

That restraint is the interesting angle. A collection grouped under this phrase is an archival prompt: name the films, locate their sources, and distinguish “withdrawn,” “edited,” “unavailable,” and “banned.” Until that work is done, the responsible description is modest but useful: a vintage-animation program organized around censorship, with its individual contents still unverified. The blank space is part of the research, not a license to fill it with folklore.

The Video That Makes Paul Simon Look Like Chevy Chase

The joke in “You Can Call Me Al” begins before the song does. Paul Simon sits beside Chevy Chase, and the video makes the taller comedian appear to own the lead vocal while Simon supplies the deadpan counterpoint. Chase lip-syncs Simon’s part; Simon mouths the backing vocal and keeps introducing instruments as if the studio has become a very small vaudeville stage.

The arrangement underneath the gag is unusually busy. Simon recorded the song at New York’s Hit Factory in April 1986 for Graceland, with a horn arrangement by Rob Mounsey and a bass line built around a descending figure played by Bakithi Kumalo. The single did not simply arrive and explode: after an initial number 44 appearance on the Billboard Hot 100, it returned and climbed to number 23 in 1987 as the album’s audience grew.

That delayed chart life fits the video’s trick. It is accessible on first glance—a famous comedian pretending to sing—but its pleasure comes from watching the construction. The camera keeps reminding us that pop performance is a set of assignments: who gets the voice, who gets the instrument, who gets to look bored. Simon and Chase turn that confusion into the entire point.
